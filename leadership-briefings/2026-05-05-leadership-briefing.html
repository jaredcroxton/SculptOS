3-Line Executive Summary

The fastest-learning team beats the most-talented team. HBR's May/June cover story by Ron Friedman identifies the single most durable edge in a fast-changing environment: not strategy, not headcount, but how quickly a team improves. Leaders who make curiosity contagious, surface problems early, and tie feedback to growth (not blame) are building the only competitive advantage that compounds. Read this before your next team review.

The human dimensions of leadership are becoming the primary moat. As AI handles more execution, McKinsey's research finds that reflective, human-centric leaders are nearly 2x more likely to believe their organisations can adapt quickly. Yet 75% of organisations are failing to build high-performance cultures. Brene Brown and Adam Grant are now weekly co-hosts on The Curiosity Shop, the sharpest new leadership podcast of 2026, exploring exactly this territory every Thursday.

Context is the leadership variable most directors overlook. HBR's Stefan Volk shows that scheduling demanding work without considering people's natural energy peaks creates unnecessary friction and missed output. Stanford's newly launched Leadership Institute is calling context-sensitivity one of the defining leadership challenges of the decade: moving from reacting to conditions toward actively shaping them.

1. This Week's Must Read / Watch / Listen — May 1–5, 2026

  • Article How to Build a Superteam That Keeps Getting Better. Ron Friedman's HBR May/June cover story reframes what high performance actually means. The teams that consistently outperform others are not the ones with the best talent or the sharpest strategy but the ones that learn the fastest. Friedman identifies three traits shared by every superteam he studied: they get more done by managing time, energy, and attention rather than hours; their members actively make each other better; and they are constantly building new skills over time. The leader's job is to make experimentation feel safe, make curiosity contagious, surface problems early, and give feedback that supports learning rather than punishing error. Compact, evidence-backed, and directly applicable to team reviews and one-on-ones. (HBR, May 1, 2026)
  • Podcast The Curiosity Shop with Brene Brown and Adam Grant — weekly Thursday edition. The most anticipated new podcast of 2026 dropped its latest Thursday episode on May 1. Brene Brown and Adam Grant (who reunited after a four-year professional rift to launch this show in March) cover the science of resilience and the pre-mortem method of failure planning, drawing on their combined bodies of research in vulnerability, organisational psychology, and learning. The pre-mortem framework asks teams to imagine a project has already failed and work backwards to identify the weak points before they materialise. Practical, honest, and delivered with the chemistry that made both their solo podcasts essential listening. New episode each Thursday. (The Curiosity Shop, Vox Media, May 1, 2026)
  • Article Tapping into Your Team's Circadian Rhythms. Stefan Volk of the University of Sydney Business School makes a deceptively practical argument in HBR's May issue: leaders who schedule meetings, feedback sessions, and complex decisions without considering when their people are naturally at their best are leaving performance on the table. Corporate culture has long favoured early-morning meetings and an implicit bias toward morning people. Volk's research shows that assigning high-stakes collaboration to shared energy peaks and protecting individuals' personal peaks for their most demanding solo work reduces friction, errors, and burnout. The actionable takeaway: map your team's chronotypes and rotate high-pressure slots rather than defaulting to 9am. (HBR, May 1, 2026)
  • Podcast Radical Candor: How Great Companies Stay Great, with Eric Ries. Kim Scott and co-host Jason Rosoff sit down with Eric Ries to discuss his new book Incorruptible (Authors Equity, May 2026), which asks why good companies go bad and what it takes to resist what Ries calls "the gravitational pull of mediocrity." Ries argues that founders start with a genuine mission but that the pressure to maximise short-term returns gradually overwrites purpose, often invisibly. The Radical Candor lens adds a useful layer: the leadership behaviours that keep companies honest (direct feedback, caring personally, refusing to protect people from reality) are exactly the same ones that resist institutional drift. A sharp hour for anyone leading a team through a period of growth-driven pressure. (Radical Candor podcast, April 29, 2026)
  • Article Stanford Launches the Stanford Leadership Institute. Stanford GSB has formally launched the Stanford Leadership Institute, backed by a foundational gift from Diane and Andreas Halvorsen and organised around five forces reshaping how leaders must operate: science and technology, environment and resources, markets and economies, government and governance, and society and culture. The institute is led by Maria Frantz (MBA 1994) and Professor Ken Shotts, and is designed as an interdisciplinary hub where faculty from across the university convene on the questions that transcend any single discipline. The guiding principle: leaders today need strategies to move from reacting to change toward actively shaping it, a distinction that is increasingly meaningful for directors in any membership or subscription business navigating shifting consumer expectations. (Stanford News, May 2026)

4. Long Reads

HBR How to Build a Superteam That Keeps Getting Better — Ron Friedman. The most actionable piece in HBR's May/June 2026 issue. Friedman synthesises research across high-performing sports teams, military units, and corporate organisations to extract what makes some teams improve exponentially over time while others plateau. The three-trait framework (efficiency of energy and attention, members actively improving each other, continuous skill-building) gives you a diagnostic you can apply to your own team immediately. Most useful read before a quarterly team review or a coaching conversation with a direct report. (HBR, May 1, 2026)
HBR Should You Appoint an Interim CEO? — Nicolas Deuschel and Robert Langan. One in three CEO transitions now involves an interim appointment, yet the authors' research shows the decision often hurts market confidence and financial performance when handled without a clear mandate. Deuschel and Langan identify four types of interim CEO suited to four different organisational conditions (board-initiated stable departure, board-initiated crisis, CEO-initiated stable departure, CEO-initiated crisis), and argue that boards which build readiness into governance before they need it make dramatically better transitions. Relevant beyond the C-suite: the same principles apply to any planned succession at director or regional-leadership level. (HBR, May 1, 2026)
McKinsey What Great Leaders Know About Not Knowing It All — David Novak. Former Yum! Brands CEO David Novak sits down with McKinsey North America chair Eric Kutcher to trace the leadership lessons from his career running one of the world's largest restaurant franchise networks. The headline insight: the leaders who get the best ideas and build the most committed cultures are the ones who stop pretending to know everything. Novak's mantra, "no involvement, no commitment," is a practical instruction for directors who want teams that execute with ownership rather than compliance. He also discusses how he managed a very public product failure by leaning into transparency rather than spinning it. A grounded, experience-rich read from a practitioner who has led at scale. (McKinsey Quarterly, late April 2026)
HBR What Companies Can Learn from Their Biggest Fans — Marcus Buckingham. Buckingham argues that the path to durable loyalty (from both customers and employees) runs through peak positive experiences rather than satisfaction with the average. He identifies five conditions that must be met in sequence for someone to move from liking to loving an experience: control, harmony, significance, warmth, and growth. Leaders who design deliberately for these conditions in their employee and customer journeys unlock deeper engagement and more sustainable performance. Particularly relevant for a membership business where the renewal decision is shaped by a small number of emotionally salient moments. (HBR, May 2026)
MIT Sloan Why Business Leaders Need to Champion Democracy — Julie Battilana, Lakshmi Ramarajan, Matthew Lee, and Vincent Pons. A timely and intellectually serious piece from a team of Harvard and MIT researchers. At a moment when many executives are uncertain about what role, if any, they should play in the face of institutional pressure and democratic backsliding, Battilana and colleagues offer four concrete options: defend democratic institutions and processes; support independent civil society organisations; limit forms of political influence misaligned with democratic principles; and foster democratic practices (voice, accountability, fair process) inside their own organisations. The fourth option is the one most directly within a director's control and the most underused. Worth a careful read this Sunday evening. (MIT Sloan Management Review, April 22, 2026)
